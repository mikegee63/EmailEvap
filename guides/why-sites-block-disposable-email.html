Why Some Websites Block Disposable Email Addresses

By Michael Goldsmith · Updated September 23, 2026

Sometimes you paste a temporary address into a sign-up form and get an error such as "please use a permanent email address". That is not a bug. Many websites deliberately block disposable email, and understanding why helps you decide what to do instead.

Why websites block temporary addresses

Free trial and promotion abuse

If a service offers a free month or a sign-up credit, a stream of throwaway addresses lets one person claim it over and over. For small businesses in particular, that cost is real.

Spam, fake accounts and bots

Communities, marketplaces and review sites depend on accounts belonging to real people. Disposable addresses make it cheap to create hundreds of fake accounts for spam, fake reviews or vote manipulation.

Account recovery and security

Services that hold money or personal data need a reliable way to reach you for password resets, fraud alerts and legal notices. An address that disappears in ten minutes defeats that, and can leave a customer permanently locked out, which then becomes a support problem.

Marketing

Some sites simply want a real address to send marketing to. That is the reason people reach for temporary email in the first place, so this is the one case where the two sides want opposite things.

How the blocking works

  • Domain blocklists. The most common method. Open-source and commercial lists record the domains used by disposable email services, and the sign-up form rejects any address at those domains.
  • DNS checks. Some forms look up a domain's MX records to see which mail provider handles it, catching new domains that share infrastructure with known disposable services.
  • Behaviour and risk scoring. Larger platforms combine the email domain with other signals, such as how old the domain is and how many accounts come from it, into a fraud score.
  • Verification and phone numbers. Requiring you to click a confirmation link days later, or to verify a phone number, filters out throwaway sign-ups without a blocklist.

What to do when a site blocks you

Respect it. Trying to get around a site's rules is against their terms, and it is against our own acceptable use policy too. Instead, pick the option that fits why you are signing up:

  • If you will keep the account: use an email alias. It is permanent for as long as you want it, forwards to your real inbox, and can be turned off later. Alias domains are much less likely to be blocked. Our comparison guide covers the main services.
  • If you only want updates from them: use plus addressing, such as yourname+sitename@gmail.com, so you can filter their mail and trace who shares it.
  • If you only wanted one download or article: consider whether you need it. Often the same information is available elsewhere without a sign-up.

Where a temporary address is still welcome

Plenty of sites accept temporary addresses, especially for downloads, newsletters, public Wi-Fi portals and communities that do not offer anything to abuse. Blocking is most common on sites with free trials, credits, reviews or payments, which are exactly the places where you should be using a lasting address anyway. Our guide When to Use a Temporary Email Address lists the good and bad cases in detail.

A fair balance

Temporary email protects a real interest: not having your address sold, spammed or leaked by a site you used once. Blocking protects a real interest too: a business not being exploited. The honest approach respects both. Use disposable addresses for the throwaway interactions they were made for, and use an alias when a site has a genuine need to reach you.
